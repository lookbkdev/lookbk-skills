When the print shop two streets away closed, its owner lent us a drawer of type for as long as we wanted it, on the condition that we use it.

We have been designing the wayfinding for a library extension, and we had drawn the letters in the usual way: on screen, at a size, with a typeface chosen for its legibility tables. The borrowed drawer made us print the same words by hand, letter by letter, and something changed in the drawing.

Wood type is never quite the same height. Some letters print heavier than others, and the counters fill in where the wood has worn. Our first instinct was to correct for it. Our second, better instinct was to notice that the irregular proof was easier to read across the room than the clean one, because the eye had something to hold on to.

We did not put worn letters on the library walls. We did open up the spacing by eight per cent, drop the weight a step, and cut the signs from oak rather than printing them, so the grain gives each letter a little of what the type had.
