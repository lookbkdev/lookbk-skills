The first bench we made for the reading room was, by every measure we had at the time, finished. The joints were tight, the oak was quarter-sawn, and the top had a 3 mm round-over on every edge, which is what we had always done. People sat on it for a week and then quietly stopped.

Nobody complained. That was the trouble. A bench that people avoid does not produce feedback; it produces a coat left on it, then a stack of books, then a sign asking you not to leave things on the bench.[^sign]

## Where the hand lands

We set a camera on a shelf and filmed ten minutes of people sitting down, with their permission and with the faces cropped out afterwards. Almost all of them did the same thing. A hand went behind them to find the front edge before they committed their weight. On our bench, that hand met a corner that was technically rounded and practically sharp.[^scale]

Watching it back was uncomfortable in the useful way. We had drawn the bench in elevation and in plan, and both drawings were handsome. Neither showed the moment of sitting down, which is the moment the bench exists for. The edge we had treated as a finishing detail was the first thing anyone touched.

> !pull The edge you can’t see is the one _you touch first._
> cite: From the studio diary, March

So the second prototype went the other way: a deep 15 mm radius that looked generous in the drawing and soft, almost upholstered, in the room. It was kind to the hand and unkind to the eye. From the door it read as a cheaper bench, the kind you find in a waiting room, where the softness is there to forgive the materials.

## Nine millimetres

The answer was not a number we could have chosen at the drawing board. We cut five test edges into a length of the same oak, at 3, 6, 9, 12 and 15 mm, and left the board on the bench in the reading room with a pencil and a card asking people to mark the one they liked. Forty-one people did. Twenty-six marked 9 mm.

![Card study models laid out on a green cutting mat beside a steel rule and a scalpel.](images/pavilion-models.jpg "Fig. 2 | Card studies on the cutting mat. The bench was not the only thing we were rounding that month.")

What made 9 mm right is that it is not one radius at all. It is a radius of 9 mm on the top face that runs out into a gentler curve on the underside, so that the edge reads crisp from across the room and feels full under the palm. Furniture makers have done this for centuries; we had simply stopped looking at it because our router bits came in single radii.[^profile]

The third prototype took eleven hours to shape by hand, most of it with a block plane and a card scraper. It is the one in the photograph at the top of this piece. It has been in the reading room since May, and nobody has left a coat on it.

## What we tell clients now

We have changed one thing in how we present furniture. Every drawing set now includes a section through the edge at full size, printed on its own sheet, and we ask clients to put a hand on it before they look at the elevation. It sounds theatrical. It is the most useful two minutes of the meeting.

We have also stopped specifying edges by radius alone. A specification now says where the hand lands, how the edge should read at three metres, and what it should feel like at three centimetres, and the maker chooses the profile that does all three. Good makers were already doing this. We were the ones holding them to a number.
